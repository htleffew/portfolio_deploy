Per-User Investigation as Trajectory, Not Snapshot

Cohort analytics and per-user investigation are different research problems with different ethical regimes. This methodology takes a flagged user account with months or years of platform activity, slices the timeline into six overlapping segments, runs multi-axis analysis on each, and produces a structured forensic report. Risk is a trajectory: the right way to characterize an account is to compare what it was doing on arrival to what it was doing immediately before being flagged.

From cohort to case

The first time I ran a per-user investigation after spending months in cohort analytics, I kept reaching for population-level tools that did not apply. Cohort analytics estimates population behavior: what fraction of users encounter a given category of content, how long the average session lasts, where the rate of policy violations is rising. The unit of analysis is the population, and the law of large numbers is forgiving of missing data and noisy signals. Per-user investigation does the inverse. It takes one account, often surfaced because automated systems flagged it for human review, and asks what specifically happened on this account, in what order, and what the trajectory implies.

The two kinds of work demand different statistical sensibilities and different ethical guardrails. Forensic per-user analysis is unforgiving on both counts, because there is no averaging across cases when the case is a single individual whose situation may end in account removal, escalation to a child-safety team, or referral to law enforcement.

The temporal segmentation

A current risk score captures the account's state at one moment. A topic distribution computed over the entire user history summarizes the whole window into one average. Neither answers the question I kept coming back to with flagged accounts: is the trajectory decelerating, stable, or accelerating? The methodology below is built around that question.

For each user of interest, the engine slices the timeline six ways and runs the full analytical suite on each slice independently.

FIG. 01 The six temporal slices applied to each user of interest. The three tertiles partition the full window for trajectory inference; the First 25 and Last 25 insets anchor the boundaries so arrival behavior is directly comparable to pre-flag behavior.

The Consolidated slice gives the baseline distribution: the user's overall content profile across the full observation window. The three tertile slices partition that window into thirds, which is the minimum granularity at which trajectory direction (decelerating, stable, accelerating) becomes statistically legible. The First 25 and Last 25 insets anchor the boundaries: the first twenty-five observations capture how the user behaved on arrival, and the last twenty-five capture how the account was behaving immediately before the flagging event. Comparing those two boundary samples directly is where I start most investigations; a user whose arrival behavior and pre-flag behavior look the same is a different finding than a user whose pre-flag behavior is qualitatively divergent from how they began.

The slices overlap deliberately. The Early Tertile contains the First 25 by construction, and the Late Tertile contains the Last 25 by construction. This lets an analyst confirm that a signal observed in a small inset is also visible in its containing tertile, which is a basic robustness check. A finding that appears only in the First 25 but not in the Early Tertile is more likely a small-sample artifact than a real signal.

Multi-axis triangulation

Each slice receives the full analytical suite from the upstream engine: time series of policy events, view source distribution, sentiment polarity, n-gram frequency, topic clusters, collocation graphs, NLTK-derived language features, and word clouds. The engine emits all of these for every slice rather than picking one or two, because each axis has known failure modes, and those failure modes are mostly orthogonal. Two signals from different axes pointing in the same direction is a much stronger inference than either signal alone.

A topic-cluster shift between Early and Late Tertiles, for example, is a candidate signal but not a finding on its own. Topic models are stochastic, the topic indices reshuffle from run to run, and apparent drift can be an artifact of corpus size differences between the slices. The same shift becomes a finding when it co-occurs with a sentiment polarity shift in the same direction and a change in the dominant view sources driving that user's content delivery. I have had investigations where a dramatic-looking topic shift turned out to be a corpus-size artifact; the sentiment axis was flat, the view sources were unchanged, and the "drift" dissolved under cross-examination. That is why the engine runs everything instead of letting the analyst pick one axis and trust it.

The view source axis is easy to skip and worth not skipping. Two users with identical topical and sentiment profiles can be in radically different risk situations depending on whether their content reaches them through search, recommendation, or direct following. A topical shift driven by deliberate search implies user agency; a topical shift driven by recommendation drift implies system-level steering. The methodology surfaces that distinction in every investigation because different remediations follow from each.

The co-occurrence layer catches what frequency and topic analysis smooth over. New word-pairings appearing at high frequency in a user's consumption window often precede new topical territory by days or weeks; the pairing is the leading indicator, and the topic shift is the lagging confirmation. The methodology weights the co-occurrence layer heavily in the Late Tertile slice, where leading indicators are most actionable.

Ethics: when does pattern inference become surveillance?

A methodology that produces forensic-grade trajectory reports on individual users is a methodology that can be misused. Four principles shape when and how it is appropriate to use.

Necessity. The investigation runs only on accounts that have already been flagged by an upstream system, with documented justification for the flag. The methodology is an interpretation tool for cases that have already cleared a threshold, not a discovery tool. Fishing expeditions are excluded by design.

Proportionality. The depth of analysis scales with the severity of the suspected violation. A spam-policy flag does not warrant the six-slice trajectory analysis; it warrants a lighter cohort-level look. The deep methodology is reserved for high-severity categories: child safety, credible threat indicators, and coordinated inauthentic behavior.

Accountability. Every investigation produces a structured artifact that is logged, attributable to the analyst, reviewable by audit, and subject to retention policy. The Jinja-templated report format from the upstream engine makes this enforceable: every section is the same in every report, so reviewers can read across investigations consistently and audit teams can verify that conclusions are supported by the documented analysis.

Redaction discipline. Reports leaving the analytics environment for legal review, policy review, or external regulator inspection are redacted by default. User identifiers, granular timestamps, and content excerpts that could re-identify an account are removed before any external transmission. The redaction is a property of the report templates themselves, which generate redacted and unredacted variants from the same underlying analysis.

These principles are the conditions under which the methodology is appropriate to use at all. A trajectory report that can support a child-safety escalation can also, in the wrong hands, be used to build a profile on someone who was never at risk. The constraints are not optional.

The investigation as input

The methodology produces structured reports, not enforcement decisions. The reports feed downstream review processes that combine the analytical output with judgments about context, intent, vulnerability, and proportionate response. An investigation report can support an account-removal decision, an escalation to a child-safety team, a referral to law enforcement, a moderator briefing for a category-level policy adjustment, or a conclusion that the flagged behavior was a false positive.

False positive disconfirmation deserves its own paragraph. Automated flagging systems have non-trivial false positive rates, and the cost of acting on a false positive at the account level is high. A trajectory analysis that finds no evidence of acceleration, no topic drift toward higher-risk territory, and no co-occurrence shifts that would support the original flag is a finding worth recording explicitly, on the same template and with the same evidentiary weight as a confirmation.

Limitations

The methodology is built for retrospective investigation of already-flagged accounts and does not generalize to real-time decision systems. The temporal segmentation requires sufficient observation history to make tertile partitioning meaningful, which excludes new accounts. Findings remain probabilistic interpretations of behavioral signals, not direct evidence of intent, and the language of investigation reports is calibrated to that limitation. Topic and co-occurrence inferences are stochastic, and the methodology relies on inter-axis convergence rather than any single axis's verdict, which protects against single-method failure modes but does not eliminate them. The redaction discipline reduces but does not eliminate re-identification risk for users in narrow demographic intersections.
