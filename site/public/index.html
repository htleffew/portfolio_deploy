Biography

I came to AI research after realizing that the methods I built to extract what a person is doing beneath what they are saying transfer almost directly to language models (and since I’m a computer nerd, I can let my geek flag fly). A large language model processes information in a high-dimensional vector space and outputs language as a translated representation of its internal states, eerily parallel to how humans use language and the dual reflection of cognition and language we see in people.

I do come by the computer geekiness honestly, both of my parents were software engineers, and we had the first home desktop computer in our neighborhood by a large temporal margin (not that I’ll date myself by telling you how large of a margin that was, but I can also tell you my dad built our first family TV, a CRT Heathkit, if that gives you a clue). I’ve also always been really drawn to psychology, so you can imagine how influential Doctor Sbaitso was for me, but not really as any kind of psychotherapist; I’m super good at testing, but I wanted to do testing at scale, and using only implicit measures, which necessarily requires computers and a good source of psychometrically relevant implicit behaviors. The most obvious path to implicit assessment is the medium of language, where content, form, deviation, and something as simple as pronoun use, unlocks all kinds of empirically validated correlates.

The computer + language + psychology thing is really nicely displayed in my PhD dissertation, since the perpetrators of mass murder cannot be interviewed or tested after the fact, I worked with what was available to farm for predictors: their pre-attack writings. I wanted to know whether the quantitative structure of that language, scored independently of its content, could predict how they would act. Two distinct behavioral typologies emerged from the clustering, and real-world attack characteristics confirmed the groupings with incredible connections to victim types, number, how the attack would end, where the attack would be perpetrated, and so on, something which offers a whole new lens for the entire field’s understanding of mass violence and the psychological states that drive it.

Apart from my obsession with psychology and computers, my first grownup professional job was working as a 911 operator and police dispatcher for the second largest sheriff’s office in the nation, serving over 19 different municipalities. That experience was seminal to how I’ve continued to operate across my whole career, not making a decision is in fact, still a decision, and decisions have consequences, and you have to learn to weigh which kind of consequences you want to have to live with and become an expert in seeing around corners. This way of approaching problems and dilemmas still rules a lot of my professional performance, where I will to the limit of reasonableness, trace down every potential downstream consequence of a decision prior to committing to it, or, as my sergeant used to say, “Just pretend everything you do is gonna be front-page news tomorrow, whadya want the headline to be?”

The “robopsychologist” self-titling is slightly ironic/tongue-in-cheek, but I do think that we are rapidly approaching a transition in the field where our AI needs their own psychologists who understand their cognitive processes, behavioral drivers, “personalities”, and everything else because if we want to pretend that this is technology we can control, we must first understand what causes it to act the way that it does, so that we can extract the predictors, and act on those before the unwanted behavior ever emerges.

If you couldn’t tell by now, I have all of the ADHD, and it’s the reason I can work on forensic psychology in the morning, AI alignment in the afternoon, and spend my evening building a retro-futuristic LLM-powered neo-noir detective adventure game ala 1989-MS-DOS-LucasArts-Nostalgia-on-Overdrive, and keep all the threads together between them (as well new fun facts I learn along the way: ask me why men have traditionally worn pants, the answer will blow you away). My side hustle during my doctorate was live club DJing, and when I am not profiling language patterns or building measurement systems, I am cutting things with giant lasers (mostly paper art), doing cloisonné badly, building furniture when the mood strikes and I figure out where my husband has hid the tools most recently, and becoming an expert at whatever I just found on YouTube.
