## Abstract

Impulsive offenders (ImO) and instrumental offenders (InO) are not mutually exclusive, and yet the neurobiological literature tends to draw a fairly steady line between them across the neuroanatomical, neurochemical, neuropsychological, and neurological categories; non-psychopathic antisocial (NPA) impulsive offending reads as largely cortical, a story of frontal-lobe dysfunction paired with heightened reactivity, while psychopathic antisocial (PA) instrumental offending reads as largely subcortical, a story of affective deficit and impaired aversive learning. Both psychopathy and antisociality seem better held as dimensional than categorical, and the distinction between a cortical and a subcortical reading is what opens a path toward type-specific intervention design.

</Band>

<Band theme="dark" spine="Clarifying Offender Ty" section="Clarifying Offender Types">

## Clarifying Offender Types

Despite only one to three percent of the male population and less than one percent of the female population being psychopathic (Pitchford, 2001), the repetitious antisocial behavior associated with psychopathy is disproportionately harmful to communities, and the fiscal societal costs of psychopathy annually exceed those of substance abuse, obesity, smoking, and other severe persistent mental disorders such as schizophrenia (Kiehl & Hoffman, 2011). Psychopathic antisociality (PA) is associated with greater recidivism, increased crime severity and variety (Porter & Woodworth, 2006), and elevated rates of violent (Blais, Solodukhin, & Forth, 2014) and sexual offenses (Hawes, Boccaccini, & Murrie, 2013).

The relationship between antisocial behavior and psychopathy has too often collapsed the two terms into one, and left in its wake the mistaken idea that antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) is simply the clinical diagnosis for psychopathy; ASPD is a highly operationalized, behaviorally oriented diagnosis that does not meaningfully include or exclude psychopathy, so it helps to keep the two apart, with antisociality as a behavioral profile and psychopathy as a psychological one, and the two free to exist independently of each other.

The Hare Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R; Hare, 1991, 2003) is the most widely used research measure of psychopathy, and even though Hare works with firm cut-off scores, factor analysis tends to surface a two-factor structure, with Factor 1 capturing the affective and interpersonal traits and Factor 2 capturing the impulsive and antisocial behaviors; taken on their own, Factor 1 scores are the better read on psychopathy while Factor 2 sits closer to antisociality (Crego & Widiger, 2015). Woodworth and Porter (2002) found Factor 1 scores positively correlated with instrumental offending (InO) and Factor 2 scores positively correlated with impulsive offending (ImO), and the same study found that 93.3% of homicides committed by PA offenders are instrumental, motivated by external, dispassionate, and often material goals.

Offenses regularly resist a clean either-or, since a violent act can sometimes serve as both means and end (Bushman & Anderson, 2001); for that reason I hold psychopathy, antisociality, and offense type as dimensional rather than categorical constructs throughout this review, and following Müller (2010) the neurobiological markers below are taken as correlational reflections of severity rather than diagnostic thresholds.

## Peripheral Nervous System and Autonomic Reactivity

Baseline readings and emotional processing in the peripheral nervous system are a core physiological component of psychopathy, and child and adult violent offenders alike tend to show low baseline heart rates (Ortiz & Raine, 2004; Scarpa & Raine, 1997) regardless of PA or NPA status; where the two groups part ways is in reactivity.

PA is positively correlated with low baseline electrodermal activity and negatively correlated with electrodermal reactivity (Herpertz, 2007), so InO perpetrators tend to hold a hypoarousal state, whereas ImO perpetrators, juveniles among them (Lorber, 2004), show increased electrodermal reactivity to stressful or aversive stimuli (Patrick, 2008). Vagal-parasympathetic heart-rate regulation and general cardiac reactivity are positively correlated with ImO across age groups (Mezzacappa et al., 1997; Peters et al., 2003; Smith & Gallo, 1999); Beauchaine, Katkin, Strassberg, and Snarr (2001) suggest that weak vagal control paired with autonomic hypoarousal produces a lowered threshold for emotionally reactive aggressive behavior, and Davidson, Putnam, and Larson (2000) read enhanced autonomic reactivity as a downstream consequence of the dysfunctional affective regulation that underlies ImO.

</Band>

<Band theme="dark" spine="Neuropsychological Con" section="Neuropsychological Contributions">

## Neuropsychological Contributions

Abnormal autonomic activity helps along the commission of violent acts, and yet on its own it does not get there; it has to be paired with some form of affective dysfunction before it issues in violent offending. PA individuals show insensitivity to punishment and a reduced capacity for fear (Lykken, 1995), where NPA individuals who engage in ImO do not show that same pattern (Porter & Woodworth, 2006), and PA individuals are unusually immune to the cognitive errors that arise from negative emotional stimuli, with under-activation in the brain regions that integrate factual and emotional information (Müller et al., 2008).

Inhibition leans on both emotional and cognitive processes, guilt and fear and empathy and a weighing of consequences among them, and what it really asks for is balance; emotional impulses cannot be allowed to overrun cognitive abilities, as appears to be the case in NPA, and executive functioning cannot be cut off from the inhibitory emotional information it needs, as appears to be the case in PA. ImO tends to travel with frontal-lobe dysfunction that hampers the regulation of threat responses, while InO follows from the absence of the moderating emotional influences such as guilt, shame, and fear (Blair, 2010; Pemment, 2013).

The startle reflex earns its place here because eye-blink responses route through a neuroanatomical pathway connecting the orbicularis oculi muscle to the nucleus of the amygdala (Davis, 1989); eye-blink startle studies show that psychopathy, though not antisociality alone, uniquely predicts an atypical lack of increased eye-blink response at longer startle latencies to aversive stimuli (Loomans, Tulen, & van Marle, 2014; Vaidyanathan, Patrick, & Bernat, 2009), and PA individuals appear deprived of the inhibitory contribution of fear because the underlying aversive learning is disrupted by subcortical dysfunction.

</Band>

<Band theme="paper" spine="Neuroanatomical Featur" section="Neuroanatomical Features">

## Neuroanatomical Features

Computerized tomography of violent psychiatric patients and homicidal offenders turns up both temporal and frontal lobe abnormalities (Blake, Pincus, & Buckner, 1995; Hirono et al., 2000; Patrick & Verona, 2007; Soderstrom et al., 2000; Wong et al., 1994), and yet only ImO perpetrators reliably show dysfunction in the prefrontal cortex (PFC; Raine et al., 1998); subcortical dysfunction, largely hypoactive for PA individuals, runs the other way in NPA individuals, who show subcortical hyperactivity, specifically in the basal ganglia and limbic regions (Amen, Stubblefield, Carmichael, & Thisted, 1996).

Hypoactive dysfunction in the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and anterior insula in response to aversive stimuli appears to carry the insensitivity to danger cues and the impairments in contextual fear conditioning found in PA (Blair, 2006; Decety, Skelly, & Kiehl, 2013; Kiehl et al., 2001; Raine et al., 2004; Seara-Cardoso & Viding, 2015); the hypoactivity reaches the amygdala as well, and contributes to the PA-typical resistance to aversive conditioning, passive avoidance learning, and recognition of fearful faces (Blair, 2010).

Magnetic resonance imaging shows that both ImO and InO offenders carry reduced functional connectivity between the left insula and the left dorsal ACC (Ly et al., 2012), between frontal cortex and temporo-limbic structures (Debowska, Boduszek, Hyland, & Goodson, 2014), and between the amygdala and ventromedial PFC (Anderson & Kiehl, 2012; Koenigs, Baskin-Sommers, Zeier, & Newman, 2011); these connections are particularly limited in PA, especially across the regions Baron-Cohen (2012) describes as the *empathy circuit*, namely the amygdala, caudal ACC, dorsal and ventral medial PFC, OFC, and middle cingulate cortex.

Both groups show increased resting slow-wave electroencephalogram activity (Patrick, 2008), and the brain-wave marker that actually tells the two apart is P300, an event-related potential involved in stimulus evaluation and decision making; reduced P300 amplitude turns up primarily in NPA offenders (Stanford, Houston, Villemarette-Pittman, & Greve, 2003) and serves as a dimensional indicator of executive dysfunction (Gerstle, Mathias, & Stanford, 1998), which sits well with ImO being the more executive-dysfunction-driven of the two groups.

<Band theme="dark" spine="Neurochemistry" section="Neurochemistry">

## Neurochemistry

The neurochemicals most often implicated in violent behavior are cortisol, testosterone, serotonin, and oxytocin; cortisol potentiates fear, builds sensitivity to punishment, and initiates withdrawal behavior, testosterone initiates approach behavior, reward sensitivity, and fear reduction, and serotonin sits at the center of mood regulation.

Gao, Glenn, Schug, Yang, and Raine (2009) suggest that the combination of increased testosterone with reduced cortisol is what accounts for the hyporesponsivity to stressors, threat cues, and punishment seen in PA; single-photon emission computed tomography has turned up abnormal dopaminergic neurotransmission in the striatum and diminished serotonin transporter density in the midbrain of NPA individuals (Tiihonen et al., 1997), and positron emission tomography work shows blunted serotonin agonist reactivity in clinical NPA populations (New et al., 2002) along with a significant negative relationship between acts of violence and serotonin-binding potential in the amygdala (Parsey et al., 2002).

Oxytocin is a powerful modulator of amygdala activity, particularly in moral decision making (Blair, 2007), interpersonal empathy (Dadds et al., 2014), and fear and anxiety (Gorka et al., 2015); PA individuals reliably show hypoactive amygdala oxytocin reception, reduced amygdala functioning during moral-decision tasks correlates strongly with InO (Blair, 2007), and methylation of the oxytocin receptor gene is positively related to the callousness and reduced emotionality typical of psychopathy (Dadds et al., 2014).

</Band>

<Band theme="paper" spine="Morphological Differen" section="Morphological Differences">

## Morphological Differences

When set against controls, both PA and NPA individuals show significantly thinner cortex bilaterally in the precentral gyri, anterior temporal cortices, left insula, dorsal ACC, and right inferior frontal gyrus, independent of age, intelligence, or substance use (Ly et al., 2012). The structural findings begin to pull the two groups apart along a few lines:

- **Prefrontal gray matter.** An 11% reduction in PFC gray matter has been found in individuals diagnosed with ASPD and convicted of violent or sexual offenses (Raine, Lencz, Bihrle, LaCasse, & Colletti, 2000); reduced frontal gray matter tracks with reduced baseline autonomic activity, though the finding is shared across both offender groups.
- **Temporal lobe volume.** Dolan, Deakin, Roberts, and Anderson (2002) reported an average 20% volume reduction in the temporal lobes of NPA individuals, without extension into the hippocampus or amygdala, which makes it one of the cleaner morphological markers of ImO.
- **Uncinate fasciculus.** Craig et al. (2009) found significantly lower fractional anisotropy, a measure of white-matter integrity, in the uncinate fasciculus, the connection between OFC and amygdala, in NPA individuals; degradation of this tract is associated with pediatric impulsivity and likely feeds adult impulsivity as well.
- **Corpus callosum.** Raine et al. (2003) found the corpus callosum to be approximately 22.6% larger in perpetrators of psychopathic instrumental offending, which points toward an interruption of the typical developmental pruning of this structure and a delayed inhibitory communication between hemispheres.
- **Acquired aggression.** Aggression acquired through damage to frontal cortex, in particular right PFC (Müller, Schuierer, Marienhagen, Putzhammer, & Klein, 2003), OFC (Blair & Cipolotti, 2000; Mitchell, Avny, & Blair, 2006), ventromedial frontal (Grafman et al., 1996), or prefrontal regions (Barrash, Tranel, & Anderson, 2000) reliably presents as reactive, impulsive aggression, not as InO; frontal lobe lesions do not always produce aggression (Mataró et al., 2001), and yet when they do, the phenotype is consistently ImO, which sits with the cortical reading of NPA and the separate, subcortical reading of PA.

</Band>

## Cortical versus Subcortical, A Summary

| Marker | NPA / ImO (cortical) | PA / InO (subcortical) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary locus | Frontal lobe, PFC | Amygdala, hippocampus, ACC, insula |
| Baseline autonomic | Low | Low |
| Autonomic reactivity | Hyperreactive to aversive stimuli | Hyporeactive, sustained hypoarousal |
| Vagal control | Weak | Closer to normal |
| Fear conditioning | Affected by inhibitory failure | Disrupted aversive learning |
| Startle reflex | Normal | Lacks increase at long latencies |
| P300 amplitude | Reduced | Closer to normal |
| Cortisol | Variable | Reduced |
| Testosterone | Elevated | Elevated |
| Serotonin | Reduced transporter density (midbrain) | Reduced amygdala 5-HT binding |
| Oxytocin | Less affected | Hypoactive amygdala reception; OXT-R methylation |
| Temporal lobe volume | ~20% reduction (Dolan et al., 2002) | Less affected |
| Uncinate fasciculus | Reduced white-matter integrity | Less affected |
| Corpus callosum | Less affected | ~22.6% larger (Raine et al., 2003) |
| Empathy circuit connectivity | Less affected | Markedly limited |
| Acquired by frontal lesion | Yes (reactive, impulsive) | No |

</Band>

<Band theme="paper" spine="Conclusion" section="Conclusion">

## Conclusion

NPA and PA can be neurobiologically differentiated from each other and from the general population across the neurological, neuroanatomical, neuropsychological, and neurochemical categories; NPA reads as a largely cortical disorder, where executive dysfunction is paired with heightened reactivity to negative stimuli, and PA reads as a subcortical disorder that produces broadly impactful affective deficiencies and learning impairments.

Holding both NPA and PA as dimensional, not categorical, conditions carries a clinical consequence worth following out, since neither group stands for persons who are categorically different from the rest of the population, and group-specific protocols become a coherent design goal once that is granted; NPA interventions would aim at improving executive functioning and neural connectivity while down-regulating affective reactivity, and PA interventions would aim at the affective deficits, the impaired aversive learning, and the limited receptivity to conditioning. The line the literature keeps redrawing is cortical for NPA and subcortical for PA, and it is dimensional all the way down.

</Band>
