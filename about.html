About

I, Robopsychologist

I love machines and I love humans. I have spent my entire career trying to understand how they think, how they interact, and what happens when they shape each other.

Both of my parents were software engineers, so I grew up in a house where computing was structural logic, something I absorbed before I understood I was learning it. I was equally drawn to psychology from the start, but not the version where you sit across from someone and ask them how they feel. I wanted to understand the gap between what a person says and what their language is actually communicating: the implicit signals people produce without knowing they are producing them.

My first professional life was in emergency communications at a sheriff's office in south Florida, and I held that job for over thirteen years alongside everything else I was building. I processed 911 calls and dispatched response units, and the work taught me something I have never been able to unlearn: when your assessment is wrong, the consequence is physical and immediate. You put a deputy in the wrong place, or someone waits who cannot afford to wait. I developed a habit during those years of tracing every downstream consequence of a decision before committing to it, and that habit followed me into every lab and research question I have touched since.

The dissertation I eventually wrote grew directly out of that obsession with implicit language. The perpetrators of mass murder cannot be interviewed or tested after the fact, so I worked with the only data available: their pre-attack writings. I wanted to know whether the quantitative structure of that language, scored independently of its content, could predict how they would act. Two distinct behavioral typologies emerged from the clustering, and real-world attack characteristics confirmed the groupings.

I came to AI research through a realization I kept bumping into during the decade I spent building measurement systems at production scale: the methods I built to extract what a person is doing beneath what they are saying transfer almost directly to language models. A large language model processes information in a high-dimensional vector space and outputs language as a translated representation of its internal states, and translations can misrepresent their source. The substrate changed, but the measurement problem did not.

The way I prefer to work is by measuring what is actually true before a team commits resources, and then making that measurement available to everyone who needs it. I have found that when a cross-functional group shares a credible picture of what is happening, the decisions get better and faster, and I spend less time arguing and more time building. I am at my best in that configuration: a small team, a hard measurement problem, and good people.

I call myself a robopsychologist because it is the closest single word for what I do. I have been measuring the gap between how a system uses language and what is actually happening underneath for twenty years, whether the system is a person or a model.

There is a thing I have not mentioned yet, which is that my brain works the way it does in part because I have ADHD, and I have never once considered that a limitation. It is the reason I can work on forensic psychology in the morning and AI alignment in the afternoon and not lose the thread between them.

When I am not profiling language patterns or building measurement systems, I am cutting things with giant lasers (mostly paper art), doing cloisonné badly, building furniture when the mood strikes, and becoming an expert at whatever I just found on YouTube.
