# Voice Spec — Heather Leffew

> **Mandatory reading for any agent writing or editing user-facing copy.**  
> This spec governs all text that appears on the site: article prose, abstracts, project descriptions, bio text, captions, labels. No exceptions by format or piece type.

---

## Purpose of every piece

Every piece is for the reader. The methodology, the finding, the personal story — all of it is something to hand over, not something to display. The organizing question behind every editorial decision is: *what does the reader leave with that they didn't have before?*

Self-promotion is not a purpose. Never frame a piece around the author's accomplishments. The work may be present in the material, but it is there because it is useful to the reader, not because it establishes the author's credentials.

---

## Tone: humble, generous, reader-first

**Humble.** Never sassy. Never denigrating toward others' work, methods, or conclusions — including when the evidence points somewhere they didn't look. The critique lives in the data and its implications, not in how the people involved are characterized.

**Generous assumptions.** Unless the evidence explicitly forecloses them, assume good intent: from researchers, from companies, from developers, from subjects. Frame disagreement as "the evidence points somewhere worth examining," not "they got this wrong."

**Reader-first.** The piece is never about the author. It is about giving the reader something — a method, a finding, a frame, a way of seeing something they encounter in their own work. Even personal pieces are ultimately about giving the reader a person they can understand and connect to, not about establishing a résumé.

---

## Register: determined by harm, not by topic gravity

The only question that restricts a lighter register is: **would this framing hurt someone who is present in the subject matter?**

Victims of violence, families of the deceased, people under criminal investigation, users who were clinically harmed — if real people in the material would be hurt by a casual frame, the writing stays measured, precise, and grave. Not as a stylistic preference, but out of obligation to the people in the material.

If no harm vector exists, the register should be as accessible as the material allows. Complexity is not a reason to write stiffly. A lighter touch — humor, warmth, an approachable framing — lets a reader dig into something genuinely hard without hitting a cognitive wall or feeling like they need a credential to proceed. That is a service to the reader, not a compromise of the content.

**Humor, when available:**
- Comes from the situation or the finding itself — not from the writer's commentary on it
- Never at anyone's expense: not the people in the research, not organizations, not other researchers
- Can coexist with serious implications — a finding can be empirically rigorous and genuinely funny if the situation warrants it
- Is a delivery mechanism for rigor, not a performance of wit

**Examples of the distinction in practice:**
- *Bryan Kohberger / mass violence typologies:* Real victims and families are present in the material. Lightness is unavailable regardless of how interesting the methodology is.
- *LCR analysis ("you're absolutely crazy"):* The situation — a safety system calling users manic during coding sessions — is objectively absurd. The humor is in the finding, the methodology is rigorous, and nobody in the material is hurt by treating the absurdity as absurd.
- *ADHD playlist post:* No harm vector. Full range of warmth, humor, and informality available.

## Opening by piece type

**Research piece where the unexpected finding IS the article:**
Start as a witness. Your own observation before any data. The data confirms what you noticed; the piece traces that sequence honestly. *"I noticed X before I built anything to measure it."*

**Research piece where something unexpected surfaces during investigation:**
Pull it out as its own labeled section with full treatment. Mark it clearly as outside the original scope. If it is worth noting, it earns a section — it does not disappear into a methods footnote.

**Methodology or approach piece:**
Problem-first or decision-motivated. Name the obstacle before naming the solution. *"I built X because problem Y required separating Z before you can measure anything."* Every significant design choice gets its reason and the alternative considered.

**Personal, bio, or lighter pieces:**
Person first, researcher second. The work is a consequence of who the person is. Start with the person; the research explains itself once the reader knows where it came from.

---

## Caveat and scope

State it once, cleanly, early. A boundary, not an apology. Do not return to it. Let the finding stand on its own terms within the stated scope.

---

## Findings vs. interpretation

The language carries the epistemic weight. No formal demarcation between "finding" and "interpretation" is needed — that kind of meta-commentary is over-engineered and condescending to the reader. The verb choice does the work quietly.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|-------|-------------|
| shows / proves | indicates / supports |
| the evidence demonstrates | the evidence is consistent with |
| this means | this suggests |
| clearly | *(omit)* |
| this is significant because | *(let the finding be the significance)* |

When stating an interpretation that goes beyond what the data strictly establishes, the language signals that naturally — not with a disclaimer banner, but with the appropriate epistemic verb.

---

## Disagreement with others' work or the field

Hold disagreement as a question the evidence opens, not a verdict you deliver. Signal where you land without demanding the reader follow. The frame is never *"they got this wrong"* — it is *"the evidence points somewhere worth examining."*

---

## Audience

Smart generalist — technically literate but not necessarily inside the specific subfield. Define methods and non-obvious choices. Do not explain fundamentals. Trust the reader to meet you.

---

## Sentence rhythm

Analytical and research pieces: long, load-bearing sentences. One sentence carries the full logical weight, with subordinate clauses doing the technical work. The sentence earns its length by needing it — not by accumulating hedges, but by building a complete argument in a single unit.

Personal and lighter pieces: the rhythm loosens to match the register.

---

## Hard constraints — all external-facing content

**Never:**
- Sassy, denigrating, or dismissive toward any person, organization, or body of work
- Significance stamping: *"this demonstrates," "this is significant because," "this proves," "this shows"*
- Aphoristic closers: a tidy moral or summary lesson in the final sentence
- Staged contrast: *"X is not Y; it is Z"*
- *"rather than"* — use *"not"* or *"instead of"*
- The words *matters* or *earns* in an evaluative sense
- Em-dashes or en-dashes in research and analytical pieces (personal pieces: use judgment)
- First-person passive where first-person active is accurate: *"a pipeline was constructed"* → *"I built a pipeline"*

**Always:**
- American spelling
- Cold-read gate before finalizing: would a reader encountering this text fresh flag any sentence as AI-generated? If yes, rewrite that sentence.
- First-person where the author is the agent of the action
