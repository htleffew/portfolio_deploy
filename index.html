Biography

Dr. Heather Leffew grew up in a house where both parents were software engineers, so computing was structural logic before it was a career. She was equally drawn to psychology, but not the clinical kind: the gap between what a person says and what their language is actually communicating. She spent thirteen years in emergency communications at a sheriff's office in south Florida, processing 911 calls and dispatching response units, and the work left a permanent habit of tracing every downstream consequence of a decision before committing to it.

The dissertation she eventually wrote grew out of that obsession with implicit language. The perpetrators of mass murder cannot be interviewed after the fact, so she worked with the only data available: their pre-attack writings. The quantitative structure of that language, scored independently of its content, predicted how they would act. Two distinct behavioral typologies emerged from the clustering, and real-world attack characteristics confirmed the groupings.

She came to AI research through a realization she kept bumping into while building measurement systems at production scale: the methods she built to extract what a person is doing beneath what they are saying transfer almost directly to language models. The substrate changed, but the measurement problem did not. She calls herself a robopsychologist because it is the closest single word for what she does: measuring the gap between how a system uses language and what is actually happening underneath, whether the system is a person or a model.
